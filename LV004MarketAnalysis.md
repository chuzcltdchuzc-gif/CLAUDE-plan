## 1. Purpose and Method

This volume characterises the market that LandVault addresses, so that strategy (LV-003)
and requirements (LV-005) rest on an accurate picture of the problem's scale, structure,
and drivers. It draws on publicly available third-party sources — government statements,
professional-services research, independent fact-checking, and press reporting — and
attributes each material figure to its source. Because published estimates of land
formalisation in Nigeria differ by methodology and date, this volume reports the range
rather than presenting a single number as settled. It focuses on Nigeria, the platform's
initial jurisdiction, while noting that the underlying conditions are common across much
of Sub-Saharan Africa.

## 2. The Land Tenure Gap

The defining feature of the market is the scale of undocumented land. The Nigerian
Minister of Housing and Urban Development stated in August 2025 that **over 97% of land
in Nigeria is not formally titled**, and that this "limits its use as collateral and
reduces its market value." Independent and press estimates converge on the same order of
magnitude from different angles: an Africa Check factsheet reports that **less than 3% of
Nigeria's land is thought to be formally registered** with federal, state, or local
authorities, and PwC's analysis found that **approximately 95% of household dwellings in
Nigeria have no title, or a contestable title.**

These figures are not identical — they measure titling, registration, and household
dwelling status respectively — but they point unmistakably to the same reality: the
overwhelming majority of land and property in Nigeria sits outside the formal, verifiable
record. The market LandVault addresses is therefore not a mature registry in need of a
better interface; it is a vast body of land whose ownership and evidence are largely
undocumented, contestable, or held only in informal and paper forms.

## 3. The Economic Cost of the Gap

The economic consequence of undocumented land is the conversion of potentially productive
assets into "dead capital" — property that cannot be readily used as collateral,
transacted with confidence, or leveraged for investment. PwC estimated in June 2019 that
Nigeria held **at least US$300 billion, and as much as US$900 billion, of dead capital in
residential real estate and agricultural land alone**, with high-value real estate
accounting for US$230–750 billion and the middle market for US$60–170 billion. PwC
attributed this dormant asset stock in large part to high transaction costs in the formal
sector and to the prevalence of untitled or contestable holdings.

For the parties in the market, the cost is concrete. Citizens cannot easily borrow
against, sell, or securely bequeath land they hold informally. Banks and mortgage
providers cannot lend confidently against collateral whose title is contestable, which
constrains housing finance. Investors and developers face elevated diligence costs and
transaction risk. Governments forgo revenue and planning capacity. And disputes are more
frequent and harder to resolve where no reliable neutral record exists to settle competing
claims. The market opportunity is the conversion of this dead capital into trustworthy,
usable form — precisely the outcome LandVault's purpose describes.

## 4. Institutional and Legal Context

Land administration in Nigeria is shaped by the **Land Use Act of 1978**, which abolished
freehold and established a nationwide leasehold system with a maximum term of 99 years. The
Act vests urban land in the state governors and non-urban land in local governments, and
the **Certificate of Occupancy** is the highest instrument of land security under it. Land
administration is therefore highly decentralised: Nigeria comprises **36 states and 774
local government areas**, each operating within its own administrative arrangements, and
multiple state-level laws govern the detail of registration and titling.

Two implications follow for any platform in this market. First, statutory authority over
land rests with government bodies, principally at state level; a platform cannot and must
not assert an authority it has not been granted, and its value depends substantially on
supporting and integrating with these bodies rather than bypassing them. Second, the
decentralisation of authority means the market is not one uniform system but many, so that
scale is achieved state by state and integration by integration rather than through a
single national switch. Both implications are consistent with, and reinforced by,
LandVault's constitutional boundaries and its kernel-first, integration-led strategy.

## 5. Demand-Side Segments

The demand for trustworthy land verification is distributed across several segments, each
with a distinct need.

**Citizens and households** need affordable, credible verification to secure homes,
access finance, and pass land to heirs. Given that the great majority of dwellings are
untitled or contestable, this is the largest latent segment by volume. **Banks and
mortgage providers** need dependable collateral verification to extend housing and
business finance; their requirement can become an anchor that mandates verification at
scale. **Developers and investors** need efficient diligence across many parcels.
**Government bodies** need more defensible and transparent records, and have signalled
intent to expand formalisation — the Minister announced a target to raise land titling to
**50% within ten years**, a policy direction that materially enlarges the addressable
market. **Licensed surveyors and firms** constitute both a supply of professional capacity
and a segment that benefits from access to work and standardised workflows. The **diaspora**
is a distinct sub-segment with strong demand for trustworthy remote verification of land
interests held at home.

## 6. Supply-Side and Competitive Landscape

Because land administration is a statutory function, the incumbent "suppliers" of the
formal record are the state land registries and the surveyor-general offices, operating
under the Land Use Act framework. Digitisation of these registries is proceeding, but
unevenly and at the level of individual states rather than nationally, which leaves the
overall record fragmented and of variable quality. Alongside the public registries, private
technology and verification efforts are emerging to address the trust and transaction-cost
gap.

This volume characterises the landscape at a high level only and does not name or assess
specific competitors, because a credible competitive teardown requires dedicated primary
research; that assessment is identified here as a follow-on deliverable rather than
asserted from incomplete information. What can be stated with confidence is structural: no
participant that merely digitises records solves the trust deficit, because a digital record
that cannot be trusted is not an improvement on a paper one. LandVault's differentiation is
therefore not digitisation but engineered trust, and its defensibility is the governed,
multi-party network described in LV-003 rather than software alone.

## 7. Market Drivers and Tailwinds

Several forces are enlarging and accelerating the market. Government policy is moving
explicitly toward formalisation, exemplified by the stated national titling target.
Continued urbanisation increases the volume and value of land transactions and the demand
for reliable records. The push to deepen housing finance depends directly on trustworthy
collateral, aligning the banking sector's interests with formalisation. The maturation of
digital identity infrastructure lowers the cost of establishing who parties are — a
precondition for trustworthy land records. And the sheer scale of dead capital creates a
standing economic incentive, across government, finance, and citizens, to convert informal
holdings into usable form.

## 8. Risks and Constraints

The same features that make the market large also make it demanding. Authority is
decentralised and statutory, so progress depends on government relationships and can be
affected by policy change; the platform's dependence on delegated authority is both a moat
and a risk. Fragmentation across 36 states and 774 local government areas means that scale
is incremental and integration-heavy. The pervasive trust deficit that creates the
opportunity also raises the bar: participants have learned to distrust records, and a new
platform must earn credibility rather than assume it. And the data itself is difficult —
undocumented, contested, and sometimes deliberately obscured — which is precisely why
fail-safe verification and honest treatment of insufficient evidence are non-negotiable.

## 9. Where LandVault Fits

Against this market, LandVault's position is coherent. The problem is a trust deficit, and
LandVault's purpose is to engineer trust rather than merely to digitise. Authority is
statutory and decentralised, and LandVault's strategy is to support and integrate with
that authority state by state, claiming none it has not been granted. Demand is anchored by
institutions — banks and government — and LandVault's go-to-market wedge is precisely
enterprise and government dispatch. Dead capital is the prize, and the conversion of
informal holdings into trustworthy, verifiable form is the platform's reason for being. The
market analysis and the platform strategy are, in this sense, two views of the same thesis.

## 10. Summary

The Nigerian land market is characterised by a formalisation gap of extraordinary scale —
on independent estimates, well over 90% of land is untitled or unregistered — and by a
corresponding stock of dead capital measured by PwC in the hundreds of billions of US
dollars. Authority over land is statutory and highly decentralised, demand for
trustworthy verification is large and institutionally anchored, and government policy is
moving toward formalisation. The central unmet need is not digitisation but trust. That is
the need LandVault is constituted to meet, and the market's structure aligns closely with
the platform's strategy, boundaries, and go-to-market wedge.
